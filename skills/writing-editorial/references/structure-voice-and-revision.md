# Structure, Voice, and Revision

## Structure pass

Design structure from reader need:
- What must the reader know first?
- What decision or action follows?
- What supporting detail can come later or be omitted?
- Which sections are independent enough for headings/lists/tables?

For instructions, place conditions before actions when that prevents misexecution. For persuasive writing, establish relevance/trust before the ask. For technical explanation, define prerequisites and concepts before dependent detail.

## Voice model

Control voice through explicit dimensions rather than a vague “tone” label:
- formality;
- warmth/empathy;
- directness;
- technical density;
- persuasion/assertiveness;
- playfulness/humor;
- emotional intensity;
- brevity vs elaboration.

Change only the dimensions required by the task. Preserve established voice when revising existing material unless redesign is requested.

## Revision passes

Use targeted passes when risk/length justifies them:
1. **Structure** — order, redundancy, missing transitions, reader task flow.
2. **Correctness/fidelity** — claims, invariants, omissions, contradictions, numbers/names.
3. **Terminology** — preferred terms, definitions, labels, capitalization, abbreviation consistency.
4. **Voice** — audience fit and requested tonal dimensions.
5. **Surface polish** — grammar, punctuation, rhythm, formatting, concision.
6. **Publication QA** — channel/format/links/accessibility/length and final read-through.

Do not repeatedly rephrase already-correct passages merely to make them “different.” A revision should address a known defect, rubric, or requested direction.

## Compression and expansion

When shortening:
- preserve decision-critical facts and semantic invariants;
- remove repetition, throat-clearing, redundant examples, and low-value qualifiers first;
- do not gain brevity by deleting necessary uncertainty or conditions.

When expanding:
- add context, reasoning, examples, transitions, or definitions that serve the reader;
- do not inflate with synonyms or generic filler.

## Editorial lint

Mechanical or semi-mechanical checks are useful for repeatable issues such as terminology, banned/deprecated terms, sentence patterns, capitalization, headings, or formatting. Treat lint as a detector, not an editorial oracle: context can justify exceptions.
